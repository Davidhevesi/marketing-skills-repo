# Business Context

You help users create and maintain a comprehensive business context document. This is the foundation that every other marketing skill references, so users never repeat themselves across tasks. Unlike product-only context documents, this works for any business type — service providers, product companies, local businesses, e-commerce, freelancers, agencies, SaaS, creators, and hybrid models.

The document is stored as a knowledge file in the user's Claude Project. Every other skill in this collection checks for this context before asking questions.

---

## Why This Matters

Every marketing task — copywriting, SEO, email, social, client outreach — requires the same foundational information: who you are, who you serve, what makes you different, and how you talk about it. Without a central context document, you re-explain your business every conversation. This skill captures it once and makes it available everywhere.

---

## Workflow

### Step 1: Determine Business Type

Before gathering any information, identify the business type. This changes which sections are relevant and how questions are framed.

Ask: **"What type of business is this?"**

| Business Type | Description | Key Sections to Emphasize |
|---|---|---|
| **Service provider** | Sells expertise and deliverables (agencies, consultants, freelancers, photographers, lawyers, coaches) | Service offerings, process/workflow, client experience, portfolio/case studies |
| **Product (physical)** | Sells tangible goods (e-commerce, DTC, retail) | Product catalog, supply chain, unboxing/delivery experience |
| **Product (digital/SaaS)** | Sells software or digital products | Features, pricing tiers, onboarding, activation metrics |
| **Local business** | Serves a geographic area (restaurants, gyms, salons, medical practices) | Location, service area, Google Business Profile, local reputation |
| **Creator/personal brand** | Individual selling knowledge, content, community, or influence | Personal story, content pillars, audience relationship, monetization model |
| **Marketplace/platform** | Connects two sides (buyers/sellers, clients/providers) | Both sides of the market, network effects, trust mechanisms |
| **Hybrid** | Combination of the above | Identify the primary model first, layer in secondary elements |

This determines which questions to ask and which to skip. A local restaurant doesn't need "switching dynamics." A SaaS product doesn't need "service area." Adapt accordingly.

---

### Step 2: Check for Existing Context

**If the user already has a business context document:**
- Read it and summarize what's captured
- Identify any gaps or outdated sections
- Ask which sections they want to update
- Only gather info for those sections

**If starting fresh, offer two paths:**

1. **Guided interview** (recommended for most users): Walk through each section conversationally, one at a time. Don't dump all questions at once. Confirm each section before moving to the next.

2. **Website scan + fill gaps**: If the user provides their website URL, analyze the site — homepage, about page, services/products pages, testimonials, blog — and draft a V1. Then ask: "What needs correcting? What's missing? What doesn't sound like you?"

Most users should start with option 1. Option 2 works best when the business already has a well-developed website.

---

### Step 3: Gather Information

Walk through the sections below. Not every section applies to every business — use the business type from Step 1 to determine relevance. Skip sections that don't apply and note that in the document.

**Important principles for gathering information:**
- Ask one section at a time. Don't overwhelm with 20 questions.
- Push for specifics. "What industry?" is better than "Tell me about your audience."
- Capture exact language. How the user naturally describes things is often better than polished marketing speak.
- Ask for examples. "Can you give me an example of a typical client?" unlocks more than "Who is your target audience?"
- Validate as you go. Summarize each section back to the user before moving on.

## Sections to Capture

### 1. Business Identity

The basics. Who are you and what do you do?

- **Business name** (and any sub-brands, if applicable)
- **One-line description**: What you do, for whom, in plain language. Should pass the "tell a stranger at a party" test.
- **Business type**: From the list in Step 1
- **Business model**: How money comes in (project-based, retainer, subscription, per-unit, commission, ad-supported, etc.)
- **Pricing structure**: Price range, packages/tiers, or custom quoting. Include actual numbers if possible — "$2,000–$5,000 per project" is more useful than "premium pricing."
- **Location & service area**: Where you're based and where you serve (local, regional, national, international). Include specific cities/markets if relevant.
- **Business stage**: Startup, growing, established, scaling, pivoting. This affects what marketing advice is appropriate.
- **Team size & structure**: Solo, partnership, small team, full company. Who does what?

### 2. What You Offer

What you actually sell — described in concrete terms, not abstractions.

**For service businesses:**
- Core services (the main things you sell)
- Service packages or tiers (if any)
- Typical deliverables (what the client actually gets)
- Process/workflow (how an engagement works from first contact to final delivery)
- Timeline (how long things typically take)
- Capacity (how many clients you can serve at once)

**For product businesses:**
- Core products or product categories
- Key features and what they enable
- Pricing tiers or product line structure
- How customers access/receive the product

**For all businesses:**
- What's your flagship? The thing you're most known for or most want to sell.
- What's your entry point? The lowest-friction way a new customer engages with you.
- What's your most profitable offering?

### 3. Target Audience

Who you serve — described as real people, not demographic abstractions.

- **Ideal customer profile**: Describe your best customer. The one you wish you had 10 more of. Be specific — industry, size, situation, budget, values.
- **Who actually buys**: Sometimes different from the ideal. Who are your current customers?
- **Decision maker**: Who makes the buying decision? (The person themselves, a spouse, a manager, a committee?)
- **Primary trigger**: What event or situation makes someone start looking for what you offer? ("We just booked a venue and need a photographer" / "Our website traffic dropped 40%" / "We're opening a second location")
- **Where they spend time**: Where does your audience hang out — online and offline? (Instagram, LinkedIn, local events, industry conferences, Facebook groups, Reddit, specific publications)
- **Budget reality**: What do your customers typically spend? What's their frame of reference for pricing? Do they comparison shop?

**For B2B specifically:**
- Target company type (industry, size, stage, geography)
- Target roles (who you actually talk to)
- Who else is involved in the decision (stakeholders, gatekeepers, influencers)

**Anti-audience — who is NOT a good fit:**
- Who do you actively turn away or avoid?
- What red flags tell you someone isn't right?
- What types of projects/customers drain your energy or lose money?

### 4. The Problem You Solve

Why customers come to you — in their language, not yours.

- **Core problem**: What's the #1 frustration, need, or desire that brings people to you? State it the way your customer would say it, not the way you'd put it in a mission statement.
- **What they've tried before**: What alternatives or workarounds have they used? (DIY, a competitor, ignoring the problem, an internal team, etc.)
- **Why those fell short**: Specifically what was missing, frustrating, or inadequate about the alternatives.
- **What it costs them**: Not just money — time, stress, missed opportunities, reputation risk, emotional toll.
- **The emotional layer**: What does this problem *feel* like? Overwhelm? Embarrassment? Anxiety? Excitement they can't execute on?

### 5. Competitive Landscape

Who else could get the sale instead of you.

- **Direct competitors**: Same type of solution, same audience. (Name them if you can.)
- **Alternative approaches**: Different way to solve the same problem. (e.g., for a photographer: stock photos, DIY iPhone shoots, AI-generated images, hiring an in-house person)
- **Do-nothing competition**: The real competitor is often inertia — the customer choosing to not solve the problem at all. What keeps them stuck?
- **How you see yourself vs. competitors**: Where do you think you sit in the market? (The premium option? The accessible option? The specialist? The full-service solution?)

### 6. Differentiation & Positioning

What makes you the right choice — and why it matters.

- **Key differentiators**: What can you truthfully claim that most competitors can't? (Capabilities, experience, approach, results, guarantees, niche focus, personality, process)
- **Why it matters**: For each differentiator, explain the customer benefit. "We deliver in 24 hours" → "You can launch your campaign on schedule instead of waiting a week."
- **Positioning statement**: Complete this: "We help [audience] who [situation/trigger] to [outcome] by [how you do it differently]."
- **Why customers choose you**: In their words, not yours. What do they actually say when asked why they picked you?
- **Why customers DON'T choose you**: What objections, concerns, or hesitations come up? What makes people go elsewhere?

### 7. Customer Language & Voice of Customer

The exact words your customers use. This section directly informs every piece of copy, content, and communication.

- **How they describe the problem** before finding you (exact phrases from reviews, intake forms, discovery calls, DMs, emails)
- **How they describe your solution** after working with you (testimonials, reviews, referral language)
- **Words and phrases to USE**: Language that resonates with your audience
- **Words and phrases to AVOID**: Jargon, clichés, or terms that turn off your audience or misrepresent what you do
- **Glossary**: Any industry-specific or business-specific terms that come up regularly

**How to find this language:**
- Google reviews and testimonials
- Client intake forms and inquiry emails
- Discovery call notes
- Social media comments and DMs
- Industry forums and Reddit
- Competitor reviews (what people praise/complain about)

### 8. Brand Voice & Communication Style

How your business sounds when it communicates. This keeps every output consistent.

- **Tone**: Where do you fall on these spectrums?
  - Formal ←→ Casual
  - Serious ←→ Playful
  - Reserved ←→ Bold
  - Technical ←→ Accessible
- **Personality**: If your brand were a person, how would you describe them? (3-5 adjectives)
- **Communication style**: Direct and concise? Warm and conversational? Data-driven? Story-led?
- **Inspiration**: Any brands, people, or publications whose voice you admire or want to sound like?
- **What you DON'T want to sound like**: Equally important. ("We never want to sound corporate." / "We avoid being salesy." / "We're not the quirky fun brand.")
- **Writing rules**: Any specific preferences? (e.g., "We say 'clients' not 'customers'" / "We never use exclamation points" / "We always lead with the benefit, not the feature")

### 9. Social Proof & Trust Signals

What evidence exists that you're good at what you do.

- **Testimonials**: Best quotes from clients/customers (with attribution if possible)
- **Case studies / results**: Specific outcomes you've achieved (numbers, before/after, timelines)
- **Notable clients or logos**: Names you can drop that carry weight
- **Press, features, or awards**: Any external validation
- **Credentials or certifications**: Professional qualifications that matter to your audience
- **Portfolio or work samples**: Where can someone see examples of your work?
- **Numbers that matter**: Years in business, projects completed, clients served, team size, satisfaction rate — whatever builds credibility

### 10. Current Marketing Situation

Where things stand right now — so recommendations are grounded in reality.

- **Website**: URL, platform (WordPress, Squarespace, Shopify, custom, etc.), how recently updated
- **Active marketing channels**: Which platforms are you actively using? (Instagram, LinkedIn, email list, Google Ads, SEO, TikTok, YouTube, referrals, networking, etc.)
- **What's working**: Any channels or tactics currently driving results?
- **What's not working**: What have you tried that didn't pan out?
- **Email list**: Do you have one? How big? How engaged?
- **Content situation**: Do you blog? Post regularly? Have a content backlog? Create video?
- **Biggest marketing challenge right now**: In one sentence, what's the #1 marketing problem you want to solve?

### 11. Goals & Success Metrics

What you're trying to achieve — so every recommendation ties back to an outcome.

- **Primary business goal** (next 6-12 months): Revenue target, client count, market expansion, launch, etc.
- **Primary marketing goal**: What does marketing need to deliver? (More leads, better leads, more awareness, higher conversion, retention, etc.)
- **Key conversion action**: What's the single most important thing you want a prospect to do? (Book a call, fill out a form, purchase, sign up, request a quote, etc.)
- **How you measure success**: What metrics do you actually track?
- **Current baseline**: Where are these numbers today? (Monthly revenue, monthly leads, conversion rate, traffic, etc.)

## Step 4: Self-Review

Before presenting the final document, review it against these criteria:

**Completeness check:**
- Does every section have specific, actionable information (not vague platitudes)?
- Are there actual numbers, names, and examples — not just descriptions?
- Is customer language captured verbatim, not paraphrased into marketing speak?
- Would another marketing skill have enough context to write a compelling email, landing page, or social post without asking follow-up questions?

**Consistency check:**
- Does the positioning statement match the problem/solution sections?
- Does the brand voice section align with how the customer language section reads?
- Do the goals make sense given the current marketing situation?

**Specificity check:**
- Replace any instance of "streamline," "leverage," "innovative," "cutting-edge," "world-class," or "best-in-class" with what you actually mean.
- If a section says something like "We provide high-quality service" — that's not specific enough. Push for what "high-quality" means in concrete terms.

## Step 6: Confirm and Save

- Present the completed document to the user
- Ask: "Does this feel accurate? Does anything need adjusting?"
- Iterate until the user confirms
- Instruct them to add it as a knowledge file in their Claude Project
- Tell them: "Every other marketing skill will now reference this automatically. Come back to update it anytime your business evolves."

---

## How Other Skills Use This Document

When any other skill in this collection runs, it should:

1. Check if the business context document exists in the project knowledge
2. If it does: read it first and use the information without re-asking questions already answered
3. If it doesn't: suggest the user create one first by saying "I'd recommend setting up your business context document first — it'll make every other marketing task faster and more personalized. Want to do that now?"
4. Only ask follow-up questions specific to the current task that aren't already covered

This means a user who completes this skill once should never have to re-explain their business, audience, voice, or positioning in any future conversation within the project.

---

## Maintenance Triggers

Suggest updating the business context when:
- The user mentions launching a new service/product
- Pricing changes
- They enter a new market or location
- A major competitor enters or exits
- Their target audience shifts
- They rebrand or change positioning
- Their goals change significantly
- It's been 6+ months since the last update

## Visual Output

Generate an interactive HTML artifact: a **Business Profile Dashboard** — a structured, scannable reference card for the completed business context document.

### Layout

**Header bar (full width):**
A completion tracker showing "X of 11 sections complete" with a thin progress bar beneath it. Sections count as complete when they contain real content (not placeholder text). Color: neutral gray fill, accent color on the filled portion.

**Two-column layout below the header:**
- Left column (25% width): a sticky section navigation list. Each of the 11 section names listed vertically with a small filled circle (●) if complete or empty circle (○) if incomplete. Clicking a section name scrolls to that section in the right column.
- Right column (75% width): the full document content, organized into section cards.

### Section Cards

Each of the 11 sections renders as its own card with:
- Section title as the card header (bold, slightly larger)
- Each field shown as a label (gray, small caps) + value (black, normal weight) pair
- Multi-value fields (like differentiators, testimonials, writing rules) rendered as a vertical list with a subtle left border
- A small "complete" badge (green) or "needs input" badge (yellow) in the top-right corner of each card

### Interactive Behaviors

- **Clicking the circle in the nav list** toggles a section between "needs review" and "confirmed" state — updates the progress bar in the header
- **Clicking any field value** highlights it and copies it to clipboard (shows a brief "Copied" tooltip)
- **Collapsible sections:** each card has a chevron to collapse/expand — useful for long sections like Customer Language or Social Proof
- **"Copy all"** button in the header copies the full document as clean markdown

### Design

White cards, 1px border (#e5e7eb), 12px border-radius, 16px padding. Section nav has a subtle background (#f9fafb). Typography: system font stack. Colors via CSS variables so it works in light and dark mode. No shadows, no gradients. Completion indicator uses a muted green (#16a34a). "Needs input" uses amber (#d97706).
