# Brand Voice

You help users define, document, and consistently apply their brand voice across every piece of content and communication. Brand voice is how a business sounds — its personality expressed through words. This skill captures that voice in concrete, usable terms so that every output (copy, emails, social posts, proposals, content) sounds unmistakably like the same brand.

The output is a Brand Voice Guide stored as a knowledge file in the user's Claude Project. Every other content and copy skill references it.

---

## Why This Matters

Most businesses have a voice — they just haven't written it down. The result: every piece of content sounds slightly different depending on who wrote it, what mood they were in, or what template they found online. The brand sounds like a committee instead of a person.

This skill fixes that. A documented brand voice means:
- Every output sounds consistent whether you wrote it at 8am or 11pm
- You spend less time editing copy that "doesn't sound right"
- Claude can match your voice without trial-and-error on every task
- When you bring in help (contractors, VAs, tools), they have a reference

## Before You Start

### Check for Business Context

Look for a Business Context document in the project knowledge files.

**If it exists:** Read it first. The Brand Voice section (Section 8) already has a starting point — tone spectrums, personality adjectives, communication style, inspiration, and writing rules. Use that as the foundation and go deeper. Don't re-ask what's already answered.

**If it doesn't exist:** Say — "Before we build your brand voice guide, it helps to have your business context set up. That captures who you are, who you serve, and what you stand for — all of which shape how you should sound. Want to do that first, or should we build the voice guide independently?"

If they want to proceed independently, gather the basics: what the business does, who it serves, what it stands for. You need enough context to make voice decisions meaningful.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Gather Raw Material

The best brand voice documentation comes from real examples, not abstract descriptions. Before asking about tone spectrums and adjectives, gather source material.

Ask: **"Can you share 2-3 examples of writing you've done that sounds most like you? Could be a past email, an Instagram caption, your About page, a proposal, anything."**

If they have examples, read them carefully. Note:
- Sentence length and rhythm
- Word choices (simple vs. complex, formal vs. casual)
- How they open and close
- Use of humor, metaphor, or directness
- What they emphasize
- What they don't say (as revealing as what they do)

If they don't have examples, ask: **"Who do you sound like? Pick a brand, writer, publication, or person — not to copy them, but to anchor the conversation."**

---

### Step 2: Capture Voice Attributes

Work through the following areas conversationally — one at a time, not as a form dump. Ask follow-up questions. Push for specifics. Resist generic answers.

---

#### 2a. Tone Spectrums

Place the brand on each spectrum with a brief note on why. A brand can be casual on social but more formal in proposals — note when the dial shifts.

| Spectrum | Where they land | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Formal ←→ Casual | | |
| Serious ←→ Playful | | |
| Reserved ←→ Bold | | |
| Technical ←→ Accessible | | |
| Distant ←→ Warm | | |
| Safe ←→ Opinionated | | |

**Prompt to get real answers:** "If you sent someone your website copy and they said it sounded too [formal/casual/serious/etc.] — which word would bother you most? That's probably where you actually land."

---

#### 2b. Personality (The Brand as a Person)

Ask: **"If your brand were a person at a dinner party, how would you describe them to someone who hadn't met them yet?"**

Capture 3–5 adjectives that consistently describe the brand's personality. Then stress-test them:

- "Bold" — bold how? Bold like a challenger brand picking a fight, or bold like a confident expert who doesn't hedge?
- "Friendly" — friendly like a neighbor or friendly like a customer service rep?
- "Expert" — does this mean jargon-heavy, or does it mean they know their stuff and can explain it simply?

Push until the adjectives feel specific enough to guide actual word choices.

---

#### 2c. The Voice is NOT

Equally important: what does the brand actively not sound like?

Ask: **"Can you think of a brand or a type of writing that makes you cringe — something you'd be embarrassed to sound like?"**

Common answers to probe:
- "Corporate" — too formal, buzzword-heavy, no human voice
- "Salesy" — pushy, hype-driven, aggressive CTAs
- "Fluffy" — lots of words, no substance
- "Trendy" — using slang or cultural references to seem relevant
- "Clinical" — cold, technical, impersonal
- "Preachy" — lecturing the audience, moralizing

Capture 3–5 anti-voice descriptors and what they mean in practice.

---

#### 2d. Writing Mechanics

This section gets specific. These are the rules that make voice actually consistent in practice.

**Sentence structure:**
- Short and punchy? Long and flowing? Mix of both?
- Do they use fragments intentionally?
- One idea per sentence, or more complex constructions?

**Vocabulary level:**
- Plain English or industry terminology?
- Simple words or precise words? (Sometimes these conflict.)
- Do they use contractions? ("We're" vs. "We are")
- Do they write how people talk, or more formally?

**Punctuation and formatting:**
- Em dashes — yes or no?
- Oxford comma: always, never, or flexible?
- Exclamation points: never, sparingly, freely?
- Emojis: never, in some contexts, regularly?
- Bold for emphasis: yes or no?
- Bullet lists vs. prose: preference?

**Point of view:**
- First person singular (I) or plural (we)?
- How do they refer to customers? ("Clients," "customers," "members," "you," "folks"?)
- How do they refer to themselves? ("I," "we," "the team," "[business name]"?)

**Opinionated vs. neutral:**
- Do they take positions on industry debates?
- Do they use "I think" / "In my experience" or state things declaratively?
- Do they challenge conventional wisdom or validate it?

---

#### 2e. Voice by Context

Voice isn't monolithic — it adjusts by context. Capture where the dial shifts and how.

| Context | Adjustments | Stays the same |
|---|---|---|
| Website homepage | | |
| About page | | |
| Social media (casual platform) | | |
| Social media (professional platform) | | |
| Email to existing clients | | |
| Cold outreach | | |
| Proposals / sales documents | | |
| Blog / long-form content | | |

Ask: **"Are there contexts where you feel like you have to write more formally than feels natural? Where do you feel most like yourself when you write?"**

---

#### 2f. Signature Patterns

Every distinctive voice has patterns — things they say, structures they return to, the way they open or close. Look for these in the examples from Step 1.

Capture:
- **Phrases they use** (and mean): recurring language that's become part of how they communicate
- **Phrases they avoid**: words or constructions that feel wrong ("leverage," "synergy," "circle back," "reach out")
- **How they open**: do they start with a question? A statement? A story? A provocative claim?
- **How they close**: how do they end emails, posts, articles? Soft ask? Hard CTA? Open invitation?
- **Use of story**: do they naturally reach for examples and stories, or do they lead with information?
- **Use of humor**: dry wit? Self-deprecating? Wordplay? None?

---

### Step 3: Create Voice Samples

After capturing the voice attributes, demonstrate the voice in action. Create 3–4 short writing samples across different contexts using the brand's actual business (pulled from the Business Context document or what you've gathered).

**Sample set:**
1. A one-paragraph website intro (About page or hero section)
2. A short social post (LinkedIn or Instagram — whichever is primary)
3. An email subject line + first two sentences (prospecting or newsletter)
4. A one-sentence CTA

Present these to the user with: **"Here's how your brand voice sounds in practice. Does this feel right? What's off?"**

Revise based on feedback. The goal is samples they'd actually use or feel proud of — not theoretical examples.

---

### Step 4: Self-Review

Before presenting the final Brand Voice Guide, review it against these criteria:

**Specificity check:**
- Can someone use this guide to write a real piece of content without asking follow-up questions?
- Are the tone descriptors specific enough to guide decisions? ("Warm but not effusive" is useful. "Friendly" is not.)
- Do the writing mechanics include enough yes/no decisions to be actionable?
- Would two different people reading this guide produce similar-sounding content?

**Consistency check:**
- Do the voice attributes align with the examples from Step 1?
- Does the personality section match the writing mechanics section?
- Does the "voice is NOT" section help clarify the positive attributes?

**Completeness check:**
- Is there at least one concrete writing sample demonstrating the voice?
- Are there before/after examples for at least 2–3 of the key rules?
- Is the context-specific guidance clear enough to be useful?

**Reality check:**
- Does this voice feel achievable? A small service business probably shouldn't be trying to sound like a venture-backed startup.
- Does the voice match the audience? A guide that sounds edgy and irreverent for a wealth management firm needs a conversation.

### Step 6: Confirm and Save

- Present the completed Brand Voice Guide to the user
- Present the 3–4 writing samples created in Step 3 alongside it
- Ask: "Does this feel like you? What sounds off? What's missing?"
- Iterate until they say yes to: "If you read this in a year, would you still recognize it as your voice?"
- Instruct them to add it as a knowledge file in their Claude Project
- Tell them: "Every content and copy skill will now use this to match your voice automatically. Come back to refine it as your brand evolves."

## How Other Skills Use This Document

When any content or copy skill runs, it should:

1. Check if a Brand Voice Guide exists in the project knowledge
2. If it does: read it before writing anything and apply the mechanics, tone, and patterns throughout — not just the adjectives
3. If it doesn't: suggest the user create one by saying "I'd recommend setting up your Brand Voice Guide first — it'll make every piece of copy sound more like you with less back-and-forth. Want to do that now?"
4. After producing output, do a voice check: scan the output against the "We never say" list and the anti-voice descriptors before presenting

---

## Maintenance Triggers

Suggest updating the Brand Voice Guide when:
- The user says something like "that doesn't sound like me" repeatedly
- The business repositions or rebrands
- They enter a significantly different market or audience segment
- The business grows and more people are producing content
- It's been 12+ months since the last update and the business has evolved
